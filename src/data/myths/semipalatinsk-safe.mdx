## The answer in 20 seconds

The Semipalatinsk Nuclear Test Site was closed by Kazakhstan in 1991. Remediation work has addressed specific contaminated zones. But the site is not uniformly safe: some areas remain contaminated, monitoring continues, and the long-term health effects on surrounding populations are still under active study by international researchers.<sup><a href="#claim-C3">C3</a></sup>

## What happened at Semipalatinsk?

The Semipalatinsk Nuclear Test Site — known in Soviet times as "The Polygon" — was the primary nuclear weapons testing ground of the Soviet Union. Located in the north-east of what is now Kazakhstan, the site operated from 1949 to 1989.<sup><a href="#claim-C1">C1</a></sup>

456 nuclear tests were conducted, including:
- **116 atmospheric tests** (1949–1963): explosions above or at ground level that produced significant fallout spread over surrounding regions
- **340 underground tests** (1961–1989): mostly contained underground, but some vented radioactive material to the surface

The atmospheric tests exposed populations in surrounding areas — including the cities of Semey (formerly Semipalatinsk) and communities in Pavlodar, East Kazakhstan, and Altai regions — to radioactive fallout. An estimated 1.5 million people lived in areas affected by fallout during the testing period.

## How was the site closed?

On 29 August 1991, Kazakh president Nursultan Nazarbayev signed a presidential decree unilaterally closing the test site — without waiting for Moscow's approval and before the Soviet Union formally dissolved.<sup><a href="#claim-C2">C2</a></sup>

This decision was driven by a significant civil society movement: Nevada-Semipalatinsk, an anti-nuclear organisation founded in 1989 by poet Olzhas Suleimenov, became one of the most powerful civic movements of the late Soviet era. The closure made Kazakhstan a central figure in global non-proliferation diplomacy.

29 August is now observed internationally as the UN International Day Against Nuclear Tests.

## Is the site safe now?

The answer depends on which part of the site and which question is being asked.<sup><a href="#claim-C3">C3</a></sup>

**Areas that have been assessed or remediated:**
- The CTBTO and Kazakhstan's National Nuclear Center have conducted extensive surveys and designated specific zones as safe for limited uses including scientific research and, in some areas, agriculture or grazing.
- Some underground test tunnels have been sealed and collapsed.

**Areas that remain contaminated or monitored:**
- Elevated radiation levels persist in some zones around former ground-zero points and underground test shafts.
- The IAEA has provided monitoring equipment and technical assistance for ongoing radiation surveillance.
- Certain areas remain restricted or require dosimetric control for visitors.

The site spans approximately 18,500 km² — an area larger than many European countries. Treating it as uniformly safe or uniformly dangerous ignores its geographic scale and internal variation.

## What are the ongoing health effects?

International research on health effects in exposed populations continues. The IARC-WHO SEMI-NUC programme is conducting long-term studies of cancer incidence, thyroid disease, and other conditions in populations that lived near the test site during the testing period.<sup><a href="#claim-C4">C4</a></sup>

Research findings have documented elevated rates of:
- Certain cancers (including oesophageal cancer and some leukaemias) in heavily exposed communities
- Thyroid abnormalities
- Possible effects on subsequent generations, though the evidence for transgenerational effects remains under study

The research is ongoing, and results from different studies have sometimes varied. This is not a closed scientific question.

## What can be said accurately

- Kazakhstan closed Semipalatinsk in 1991 in an act of significant political courage.<sup><a href="#claim-C2">C2</a></sup>
- Remediation work has addressed specific contaminated zones, and parts of the site are safe for defined uses.<sup><a href="#claim-C3">C3</a></sup>
- Other zones remain contaminated and require monitoring.
- Health effects on affected populations are real, documented, and continue to be studied.<sup><a href="#claim-C4">C4</a></sup>
- "Completely safe" and "still an emergency" both misrepresent a complex, spatially variable situation.<sup><a href="#claim-C5">C5</a></sup>

## Where this claim appeared

- **Tourism materials and travel blogs** — Some texts promoting tourism to the Semey region describe the test site as "fully remediated" or "safe to visit" without noting ongoing monitoring requirements
- **Reddit r/geography and r/history** — Questions about Semipalatinsk frequently produce responses saying it is "cleaned up now" with no mention of ongoing research ([reddit.com/r/history](https://www.reddit.com/r/history/))
- **The Guardian (2011)** — A piece on Central Asian nuclear legacy quoted officials describing remediation progress without noting the site's scale and remaining contaminated zones ([theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com))
- **Vice News (2017)** — A documentary on Semipalatinsk took the opposite approach, framing the entire region as still acutely dangerous — equally misleading in the other direction ([vice.com](https://www.vice.com))
- **Wikipedia "Semipalatinsk Test Site"** — The article is generally accurate but has been repeatedly simplified in edits to imply either full safety or ongoing emergency; the current version reflects the nuanced picture more accurately
